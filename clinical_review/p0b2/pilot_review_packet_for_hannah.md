# Genex Pilot — developmental steps for review

Hannah, this is the small set of decisions Genex needs from you before it adapts activities between developmental steps. Everything here reuses the existing CDC milestones and Genex bridge milestones — nothing is a new milestone. Please mark each item **Approve / Edit / Reject** (or choose a step where asked).

How it works for a family: Genex picks activities for the child's current step. If an activity is rated *too hard* on two different practice days, the next week moves **one step down**; *too easy* on two different days moves **one step up**. A single rating never moves the child — the card's "make it easier / make it harder" tips cover the moment. Families never see step names.

### C1. Who's Here  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Naming familiar people
- **Materials:** A few photos of familiar people your child sees often — for example Mama, Papa, a sibling, grandparent, or caregiver. Photos can be kept on a phone or placed in a small photo album.
- **Instructions:** Choose two or three photos of people your child knows well. Sit together and look at one photo at a time. Point to the person and say the name your family uses for them once, such as "Mama." Pause for a few seconds and give your child a chance to respond. Accept any sound, approximation, or word that seems connected to that person, then warmly say the name again. Look through the same few photos regularly so your child gets repeated opportunities to hear and try the names. You can also practice naturally when the person is nearby or enters the room: point them out, say their name once, and pause to give your child a chance to respond.
- **Success looks like:** The child makes a sound, word approximation, or word that appears connected to one of the familiar people at least once.
- **Make it easier:** Start with just one very familiar person and use the same photo repeatedly. If your child looks at, points to, smiles at, or reaches toward the person in the photo without making a sound, acknowledge the response and model the name again.
- **Make it harder:** Add a second or third familiar person and give your child opportunities to hear and attempt different names.
- **What to avoid:** Do not quiz your child with repeated "Who is this?" questions or ask them to repeat the name over and over. Model the name, pause, and keep the interaction relaxed and playful.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet. The drafting note refers to the 12-month first-words milestone.

### C2. Peekaboo Names  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Naming familiar people during a playful social game
- **Materials:** No materials needed. A doorway, hands, a light scarf, or a mirror can be used.
- **Instructions:** Choose one person your child knows very well, such as Mama, Papa, a sibling, or a grandparent. Have that person briefly hide behind a doorway, their hands, or a light scarf. When the person appears, point to them and say their familiar name once: "Papa!" Pause for a few seconds and give your child a chance to respond. Accept any sound, approximation, or word that seems connected to the person, then say the name warmly again. Repeat for a few rounds while your child is enjoying the game. Optional mirror variation: sit or stand together in front of a mirror. Point to yourself in the mirror and say the familiar name your child uses for you, such as "Mama." Pause and give your child a chance to respond, then say the name again. If another familiar person joins, take turns pointing to each person in the mirror and saying their name. Keep the focus on familiar-person names rather than asking the child to identify themselves.
- **Success looks like:** The child makes a sound, word approximation, or word that appears connected to the familiar person during at least one round.
- **Make it easier:** Use the same familiar person for every round and keep the hiding very brief. Say the person's name as soon as they appear.
- **Make it harder:** Later, play with a second familiar person so your child has opportunities to hear and attempt two different names.
- **What to avoid:** Do not ask "Who is it?" over and over or require your child to say the name before the person appears again. Stop if your child loses interest or seems uncomfortable with the hiding.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet.

### C3. Favorite Things  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Naming familiar favorite objects
- **Materials:** Two or three favorite toys or familiar objects your child knows well
- **Instructions:** Place two or three familiar favorite items in front of your child, such as a ball, car, doll, book, or cup. Pick up or point to one item and say its name slowly once. Pause for a few seconds and give your child a chance to make a sound, approximation, or word. Accept any attempt that seems connected to the item, then say the full word warmly and let your child play with or hold the item. Repeat with the other items, following your child's interest rather than going through them like a test.
- **Success looks like:** The child makes a sound, word approximation, or word that appears connected to at least one familiar object.
- **Make it easier:** Start with just one or two very familiar items. You can focus first on understanding: say "Give me the ball," or "Where's the ball?" and let your child look, point, reach, or hand you the correct item. Then model the word yourself — "Ball!" — and pause to give your child a chance to make any sound or word attempt. A correct look, point, or hand-over shows participation and understanding, but does not count as spoken-word success.
- **Make it harder:** Increase the choice to three or four familiar objects, or add one new object alongside words your child already knows. Give your child a little more time to start the word before you model it.
- **What to avoid:** Do not repeatedly ask "What is this?" or require the word before your child can have the toy. Keep it playful and model the word yourself if your child does not attempt it.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet.

### C4. Snack Choices  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Using first words to choose between familiar foods
- **Materials:** Two familiar snacks your child likes
- **Instructions:** At snack time, show your child two familiar choices, such as banana and crackers. Hold or place both where your child can see them and name each one once: "Banana. Crackers." Ask a simple choice question such as, "Which one do you want?" Give your child a few seconds to look, point, reach, make a sound, or attempt a word. When your child chooses, say the name of the chosen snack again — "Banana!" — and give it to them. If your child makes a sound, approximation, or word connected to the snack, accept it warmly and model the full word once. Repeat naturally during the snack without turning every bite into a language trial.
- **Success looks like:** The child makes a sound, word approximation, or word that appears connected to one of the snack choices at least once.
- **Make it easier:** Use only two very familiar choices. Let your child choose by looking, pointing, reaching, or touching. Name the snack they choose and pause briefly to give them a chance to make a sound or word attempt. Choosing correctly shows communication and understanding, but does not need to count as spoken-word success.
- **Make it harder:** Offer three familiar choices, or give your child more time to try the snack's name before you model it. If your child already attempts some food words, encourage them to use the word while choosing — but still give the chosen food whether or not they say it.
- **What to avoid:** Never make food depend on saying a word. Do not withhold a snack, repeatedly demand "Say ___," or require correct pronunciation before giving the food. Keep the interaction relaxed and let the choice remain meaningful.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet.

### C5. Bath and Bedtime Words  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Naming familiar objects during everyday care routines
- **Materials:** Familiar items already used during bath time or bedtime
- **Instructions:** Choose one familiar item your child sees often during the routine, such as a duck, towel, pajamas, toothbrush, or book. When you naturally reach for the item, say its name once and pause briefly to give your child a chance to respond. Accept any sound, approximation, or word that seems connected to the item, then warmly model the full word again and continue the routine. Use the same word for several days so your child gets repeated opportunities to hear and try it.
- **Success looks like:** The child makes a sound, word approximation, or word connected to the familiar item at least once during the routine.
- **Make it easier:** Use one highly familiar item and say its name twice before a short pause. Keep the interaction brief and predictable.
- **Make it harder:** Add a second familiar item from the routine and give your child opportunities to hear and attempt both words across the week.
- **What to avoid:** Do not turn bath time or bedtime into a test, delay sleep, or repeatedly ask your child to say the word. During bath time, never pause or change what you are doing in a way that interferes with close supervision and safety.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet.
- **Needs your explicit decision:** the bath-time supervision sentence in *What to avoid* was drafted outside clinical review — approve, replace, or supply standing wording.

### C6. Magic Word Box  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Discovering and naming familiar objects
- **Materials:** A small box or bag and two or three familiar objects your child likes — for example toy cars, stuffed animals, bath toys, or favorite everyday objects
- **Instructions:** Choose two or three familiar objects and hide them inside a box or bag. Let your child reach in and pull out one object at a time. When it appears, react with interest and say its name once: "Car!" Pause for a few seconds and give your child a chance to make a sound, word approximation, or word. Accept any attempt connected to the object and warmly say the full word again. Let your child hold or play with the object before reaching into the box for the next surprise. Repeat with the same small group of objects so the words become familiar.
- **Success looks like:** The child makes a sound, word approximation, or word connected to at least one object.
- **Make it easier:** Start with only one or two highly familiar objects. Name each object as soon as it comes out and let your child explore it. Pause briefly after saying the word to leave room for any sound or word attempt.
- **Make it harder:** Use three or four familiar objects. Before naming the object yourself, pause and give your child a chance to start the word. You can also use objects from the same group, such as a car, bus, and truck, or several familiar animals.
- **What to avoid:** Do not repeatedly ask "What is this?" or require your child to name the object before playing with it. Keep the surprise and play more important than getting the word right.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet.

### C7. Match the Picture  ☐ Approve ☐ Edit ☐ Reject — step: ☐ Step 1 ☐ other: ____

- **Theme:** Matching familiar objects with their pictures and hearing their names
- **Materials:** Two or three familiar objects and a clear photo of each one. For the easiest version, take photos of the child's actual objects. Make each photo simple and easy to recognize: show one object by itself, from a similar angle, with similar colors to the real object. Photos may be printed or displayed one at a time on a phone.
- **Instructions:** Start with two familiar objects and their matching photos. Place the two photos in front of your child. Hand your child one of the real objects, say its name once — "Ball!" — and help them look for the matching picture. When they find or touch the matching photo, say the word again: "Ball!" Pause for a few seconds and give your child a chance to make a sound, approximation, or word. Accept any attempt and warmly model the full word. Repeat with the other object.
- **Success looks like:** The child matches or shows recognition of at least one familiar object and makes a sound, word approximation, or word connected to one of the objects. The match itself shows understanding; the sound or word attempt is the expressive-language part of the activity.
- **Make it easier:** Use only two very familiar objects. Take photos of the actual objects your child will match, with a simple background and as little visual difference as possible between the photo and the real object. If matching is difficult, place the real object beside its photo yourself, say the name, and let your child explore both. The child does not need to match independently at first.
- **Make it harder:** Add a third or fourth object and picture. Once matching is easy, try using a different photo of the same kind of object — for example, your child's red ball matched to a picture of another ball — while continuing to model the same word. You can also pause before saying the name to give your child a chance to attempt it first.
- **What to avoid:** Do not make matching accuracy the main goal or repeatedly correct mistakes. If your child is unsure, help them match the object and picture, say the word, and keep the activity moving. The purpose is repeated, meaningful exposure to familiar first words.
- **Origin:** founder-drafted, no clinician input yet. The drafting note refers to the 18-month first-words milestone.
